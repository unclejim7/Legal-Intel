The AI Workplace Injury Liability Triangle: Employer, Robot Manufacturer, Software Vendor

The BLS data on robot-related workplace injury growth — 34% year-over-year — has not been matched by legal framework development. When a warehouse picker is injured by an autonomous mobile robot, three liability theories run simultaneously: employer negligence in deployment and training; robot manufacturer product liability for design or manufacturing defect; and AI software vendor liability for algorithmic decision errors that caused the unsafe movement or action.

Illinois WC practitioners handling warehouse and manufacturing injuries should be running all three theories in parallel from the first client meeting. The WC claim is the immediate recovery vehicle, but the third-party tort against the manufacturer or software vendor is where the damages ceiling is. Document the robot's ID, manufacturer, software version, and the specific task being executed at the time of injury. That information is often available from the employer's warehouse management system and is frequently not preserved after the incident without a litigation hold letter.

Carrier AI Fraud Detection in Discovery: Turning the Surveillance System Against Itself

Insurance carriers use AI fraud detection systems to flag WC and PI claims for investigation — scoring claims for statistical anomaly, running social media surveillance, and cross-referencing prior claim history. These systems generate reports, scores, and flags that are typically retained in the carrier's claims management system and are discoverable in a bad faith action or in any matter where the carrier's claims handling is at issue.

The discovery demand that plaintiff WC attorneys should be adding to every bad faith and disputed claim matter: "Produce all AI-generated fraud scores, flags, or investigation referrals generated in connection with this claim, including the model used, the inputs, the output, and any human review of the AI recommendation." Carriers frequently resist this production as proprietary. Push back — the claim handling process is not a trade secret when it's the basis for a benefit denial.

⚖ Legal Fact of the Day
Under the Illinois Workers' Compensation Act, an employer who fails to carry required workers' compensation insurance is subject to a penalty of up to $500 per day of noncompliance — and an injured worker may sue the uninsured employer in civil court without the exclusive remedy bar that normally shields employers from tort liability. Illinois has one of the most aggressive uninsured employer penalty structures in the country.
📖 Word of the Day
Comparative fault (n.) — A tort doctrine apportioning liability between plaintiff and defendant based on each party's relative degree of negligence. Illinois follows a modified comparative fault rule under 735 ILCS 5/2-1116: a plaintiff who is more than 50% at fault is barred from recovery entirely; a plaintiff who is 50% or less at fault recovers damages reduced proportionally by their percentage of fault. Critical in PI cases with contributory negligence defenses.
