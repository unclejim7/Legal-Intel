Colorado's AI Employment Law Dies Before Birth — And the Template It Leaves Behind

Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed SB 26-189 on May 14, 2026, gutting and replacing the state's landmark Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act two weeks before its June 30 effective date — the most comprehensive AI employment law in the country reduced to a narrow notice-and-recordkeeping regime before a single enforcement action was ever filed. The original Act, which would have required employers to conduct impact assessments, audit AI systems for algorithmic discrimination, and maintain annual compliance reviews, was simultaneously stayed by a federal magistrate judge on April 27 after an AI developer filed constitutional challenges and the DOJ intervened arguing the law unconstitutionally compelled AI systems to adopt state-defined viewpoints.

For workers' compensation and personal injury attorneys, the death of the original Colorado Act is a two-sided development. On the employer and carrier side, the heat is dramatically lower — the replacement law, effective January 1, 2027, requires only pre-use notice when automated decision-making technology influences consequential employment decisions (hiring, promotion, compensation, discipline) and an adverse action disclosure process. No mandatory bias audits. No algorithmic impact assessments. But the pattern matters: Illinois's HB 3773 has been operative since January 1, and its broader prohibition on AI that produces discriminatory employment effects is very much alive — creating a discovery theory for plaintiff WC attorneys every time a carrier uses AI-assisted claims scoring on an injured worker's file.

The practical read for WC/PI practitioners: Colorado's retreat narrows immediate compliance burdens, but the underlying AI accountability infrastructure that plaintiff lawyers were preparing to exploit — the audit trails, impact assessments, and explainability mandates — now only clearly exists in Illinois. Carriers deploying AI claim valuation systems in Illinois should be preparing for interrogatories about how those systems work. Plaintiffs' counsel should start asking.

Reporter's Take: Colorado's law didn't fail because AI accountability was a bad idea — it failed because it was written before anyone built the compliance infrastructure to implement it. The replacement isn't the end of AI employment law; it's the prototype of what survives judicial and political pressure. Illinois's version will be the test case for the next three years.

◼ Editor's Dispatch — Quick Takes
The Colorado Retreat Is Not the End of AI Employment Law — It's the Editing Process
Colorado's original AI Act tried to do everything at once: bias audits, impact assessments, annual reviews, remediation mandates. It collapsed under its own weight before taking effect. What survived — notice, adverse action disclosure, recordkeeping — is the compliance skeleton that every subsequent AI employment law will be built on. Illinois HB 3773 has substantially the same architecture. The question for WC/PI practitioners is not whether AI employment law survives, but how the notice and recordkeeping requirements translate into discovery obligations in contested claims where AI-assisted decisions are challenged.
Harvey's 500 Agents Are a Map of What Doesn't Exist Yet for WC/PI
Browse Harvey's pre-built agent library and you are looking at the entire legal AI build roadmap for BigLaw practice areas — M&A, due diligence, IP, fund formation. What you won't find: anything built for injured workers, IME disputes, medical causation timelines, lien resolution, or settlement valuation. This is not oversight; it's economics. BigLaw pays more per seat and closes deals faster. But the WC/PI market is $61.7 billion and structurally underserved by enterprise AI. The first firm — or the first founder — to build a Harvey-quality agent stack for workers' comp will have first-mover advantage in a market where 55% of adjusters are already overwhelmed and 60% of PI firms are looking for something better than what they have.
The AI Explainability Problem Is Plaintiff Counsel's Best New Discovery Theory
Carrier AI claim valuation systems are making consequential decisions — reserve-setting, denial recommendations, settlement authority thresholds — on the basis of algorithmic analysis that, in many cases, cannot be reconstructed after the fact. When a WC claim reaches litigation and the adjuster's decision trail leads back to an AI output, plaintiff counsel now has a coherent theory: demand production of the AI system documentation, the claim scoring outputs, and the decision log. If the carrier can't produce an auditable trace — and most can't yet — that's a spoliation argument in the making. The Wisedocs piece published this week is the best available primer on why the explainability gap is not just a compliance problem; it's a litigation exposure.
