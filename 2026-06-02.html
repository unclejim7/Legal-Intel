Colorado Kills Its AI Landmark — And What Rises in Its Place Should Alarm Insurers

Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed SB 26-189 on May 14, repealing and replacing the nation's most ambitious AI consumer protection law — the Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) — just 46 days before its scheduled June 30 effective date. The replacement, dubbed the Colorado Automated Decision-Making Technology Act (CADMA), doesn't take effect until January 1, 2027, and dramatically narrows the compliance architecture: gone are broad "algorithmic discrimination" mandates, impact assessments, and comprehensive governance frameworks. What remains is a targeted regime focused on consumer disclosures, post-adverse-outcome explanations, and meaningful human review where AI "materially influences" a "consequential decision." For workers' compensation and personal injury attorneys, the word insurance sits squarely in CADMA's definition of consequential decisions — meaning carriers using AI to value claims, deny treatment, or triage settlements will face disclosure and human-review obligations in Colorado starting next year, and likely in every state watching this model spread.

Reporter's Take: Colorado just handed the insurance industry a temporary reprieve while simultaneously writing the blueprint that plaintiffs' attorneys will use nationwide — CADMA's insistence on human review and adverse-outcome explanation rights is precisely the discovery hook that will crack open carrier AI black boxes in litigation.

ETHICS — ABA

ABA Formal Opinion 512 Remains the Governing Standard

ABA Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) continues as the primary national ethics framework for generative AI use by attorneys. The opinion requires competent understanding of AI capabilities and limitations, independent verification of all AI-generated output, and confidentiality compliance. State bars are accelerating their own supplemental guidance: 75% of U.S. lawyers now use AI, but only 25% have received formal ethics training on AI use — a gap creating significant malpractice exposure for firms that have deployed AI tools without formal governance policies.

WC/PI Angle: Every AI-drafted motion, demand letter, or brief must be independently verified against primary source records before filing. "The AI told me so" is not a defense to Rule 11 sanctions or an ARDC complaint.

Carrier AI Claim Valuation: The Pressure on Soft-Tissue and Psychological Harm Claims

Major insurers' AI-driven bodily injury valuation platforms — which cross-reference comparable case outcomes, medical bill databases, and injury type classifiers — are being documented as systematically producing lower ranges for soft-tissue injuries and psychological harm claims. Consumer advocates in California have noted that these systems output "recommended settlement ranges" carrying implicit authority that pressures unrepresented claimants to accept less than fair value. For PI attorneys: When opposing counsel is a carrier deploying algorithmic valuation, understanding how the system weights inputs (treatment duration, provider type, claimed limitations) is becoming as important as understanding the human adjuster. The practical counter is attorney representation itself — carriers' own predictive systems apparently flag represented claimants as higher litigation risk, shifting the internal calculus toward more serious evaluation.

AI Medical Record Review: The Hallucination Problem and the Source-Link Rule

Multiple 2026 practitioner guides are now converging on a single non-negotiable standard for AI-assisted medical record review: every AI-extracted fact must link back to a specific page — and preferably a specific text region — in the source record. Tools that produce extraction without source links are not production-ready for medical records, regardless of claimed accuracy rates. The risk is asymmetric: a single fabricated timeline entry doesn't just invalidate that entry — it gives defense counsel grounds to challenge the entire AI-generated chronology at deposition or arbitration. For WC/PI firms evaluating AI tools: Source-link verification capability is now the threshold requirement, not a premium feature.

Insurer AI Fraud Detection: What It Flags and Why It Matters for Claimant Counsel

AI fraud detection systems deployed by WC carriers are now using behavioral pattern analysis to flag claims where a provider "routinely bills the same procedure across unrelated claims" or where a claimant shows "inconsistent medical history across jurisdictions." These systems are being trained on historical claims data to recognize statistical anomalies — not individualized medical judgment. For plaintiff-side WC attorneys, understanding the triggering logic of these systems matters: a client who treats with a high-volume provider or has prior claims in multiple states may face elevated scrutiny regardless of the legitimacy of the current claim. Counsel should proactively document the treating provider's methodology and billing consistency to blunt AI-generated red flags before they harden into denial decisions.

📖 Word of the Day
Algorithmic Discrimination (n.) — The use of an automated decision-making system in a way that produces disparate or prejudicial outcomes against individuals based on protected characteristics, even without discriminatory intent. The concept sits at the intersection of civil rights law and AI regulation; Illinois's HB 3773 and Colorado's repealed AI Act both addressed it directly, and the doctrine is migrating from employment law into insurance claim handling, WC benefit administration, and settlement valuation — where carrier AI systems are now being scrutinized for discriminatory output patterns.
