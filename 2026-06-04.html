Colorado Kills Its Own AI Law Before It Takes Effect — And Illinois Fills the Vacuum

The nation's most-watched AI employment law just died before it could live. Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed SB 26-189 on May 14, 2026 — a wholesale replacement of the state's landmark 2024 Colorado AI Act, which had been scheduled to take effect June 30. The original law would have imposed bias audits, full risk management programs, and algorithmic discrimination assessments on any employer deploying AI in hiring, promotion, or termination decisions. After sustained pushback from the tech industry, a constitutional challenge filed by xAI (joined by the DOJ), and a federal magistrate's April 27 stay, the Colorado legislature passed the rewrite in the final days of its session. The replacement trades sweeping governance obligations for a narrower transparency-and-notice framework, pushes the new effective date to January 1, 2027, and limits enforcement to the attorney general — no private right of action. Back in Chicago, however, the Illinois Department of Human Rights is moving in the opposite direction: its proposed Subpart J regulations under HB 3773 — which took effect January 1, 2026 — remain on track, with a written public comment window closing June 29, 2026, even after the June 10 hearing was postponed on June 2 for "continued collaboration with other state agencies."

Reporter's Take: Colorado's retreat is a tactical win for employers deploying AI-assisted return-to-work tools, vocational rehab platforms, and any carrier using automated claims triage — but Illinois attorneys should not read this as deregulatory momentum. The IDHR is writing rules with teeth, the comment window is still live, and any workers' comp or PI firm whose carrier-clients use AI in claims decisions needs to be watching this closely.

◼ The Agentic Corner

Lavern Goes Open Source — And the Capability Floor Drops to Zero

When Finnish legal technologist Antti Innanen published his April 7 essay "I Built an Agentic Law Firm, Now What?" on Artificial Lawyer, the most striking thing wasn't the 66 agents or the Mac Mini. It was the question at the end: should he open-source it or find a buyer? He gave it 30 days. The answer, published in late May: Apache 2.0, free to the world, now expanded to 67 specialist agents.

The architecture is worth understanding. Lavern runs entirely on-device — no cloud, no external data exposure. It operates on a 30-minute heartbeat in "Clawern mode," monitoring inbound matters, distributing work to specialist agents, having those agents debate and check each other's output through a 10-pass verification loop, and surfacing reviewed results without a human initiating any step. A Partner agent handles intake, routes tasks to specialists, and manages internal four-eyes review. Governance is built in: human approval gates are mandatory before any output leaves the sandbox, and the system never modifies original documents.

Innanen himself is careful: "The architecture is real and the code is open. What hasn't been independently validated is the quality bar." For WC/PI firms, the practical implication is narrower than the headline suggests. A system of 67 agents running continuously is not a solo practitioner's immediate deployment path. But the component ideas — a 30-minute monitoring heartbeat that surfaces what needs human attention, agents that cross-check each other's output, strict sandbox operation on client documents — are all deployable concepts that will appear inside commercial products within 18 months. Clio Work's agentic features and Microsoft 365 Copilot are already delivering limited versions of this at scale. The question for WC/PI firms right now isn't whether to deploy Lavern — it's whether they've documented their workflows well enough to hand them off when the time comes.

The June 29 IDHR Comment Deadline Is the Most Important AI Date on the Illinois WC Calendar

With the IDHR's public hearing postponed, the written comment window — closing June 29 — is now the only formal mechanism for practitioner input on Subpart J. The proposed regulations go further than HB 3773's text in several respects: they require notice to an employee's bargaining representative (not just the individual employee) when AI is used in employment decisions, and they extend employer obligations to third-party agents and vendors acting on the employer's behalf.

For WC/PI attorneys, the practical exposure is indirect but real. Carriers using AI to evaluate return-to-work (RTW) suitability, assess job-fit, or triage claim severity are deploying AI in exactly the space HB 3773 regulates. If a claimant's employer or insurer uses an AI-driven RTW tool without proper notice — and that tool produces a discriminatory outcome based on protected class or a zip-code proxy — there is a viable IHRA claim on top of the underlying WC matter. Illinois WC practitioners should be counseling clients on this layered exposure now, before final rules are issued.

◼ Medical & Expert Intelligence

AI-Enhanced IME Imaging Raises the Evidentiary Stakes on Both Sides

A February 2026 analysis from CDCiME on imaging advances in IME practice documents the shift: AI-enhanced MRI and 3D reconstruction technology is giving IME physicians higher-resolution, more objective imaging data to support — or challenge — causation, extent of injury, and treatment necessity. Subtle pathology like ligament microtears and early disc degeneration that previously required specialist radiologist review is now surfaceable through AI pattern recognition at the point of the IME itself.

For plaintiff counsel, this cuts both ways. AI-enhanced imaging that confirms injury extent can strengthen causation arguments significantly, particularly in soft-tissue cases where "nothing shows on the MRI" has historically been a defense staple. But IME physicians equipped with AI imaging analysis may produce more technically defensible impairment ratings — ratings harder to attack on the grounds of superficial examination or inadequate review of records.

For defense counsel and carriers, the QA integration noted by CorVel — where AI tools pre-check IME reports for consistency, completeness, and internal coherence before release — reduces the risk of sloppy reports that get shredded on cross. The downside: if the AI QA system flags and corrects a report that was initially inconsistent, that audit trail may be discoverable.

The Daubert/Frye admissibility question is moving from theory to practice. Courts are beginning to encounter AI-generated imaging analysis and AI-assisted impairment ratings. Proprietary algorithm opacity is the core vulnerability: if a vendor won't disclose training data or methodology, cross-examination of the underlying AI tool becomes both viable and necessary.
