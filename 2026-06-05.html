◼ ABOVE THE FOLD

NINTH CIRCUIT SUSPENDS TWO ATTORNEYS SIX MONTHS FOR AI HALLUCINATIONS — AND THEIR COVER-UP

The most significant AI discipline order in federal appellate history landed Wednesday when the Ninth Circuit suspended California immigration attorneys Mike Singh Sethi and William Rounds for six months from practicing before the court, fined each $2,500, and ordered mandatory AI-use disclosure in all future filings for two years. The case, LNU v. Blanche (No. 24-4790), arose from briefs containing fabricated case citations that don't exist — including "Eduardo v. Garland, 28 F.4th 742 (9th Cir. 2022)" and "Lay v. Holder, 729 F.3d 962 (9th Cir. 2013)" — plus misattributed quotations from real opinions. What elevated the misconduct from negligence to discipline: the attorneys repeatedly denied AI involvement, called the errors "typographical mistakes," and compounded the deception at every subsequent procedural opportunity — through a motion to correct, oral argument, and response to the order to show cause. The court identified a second, harder-to-detect category of AI error — "inaccuracies" where real cases are cited but their holdings are misstated — calling these potentially more dangerous than outright fabrications because they survive cursory checks. Citing a 2024 study finding that Westlaw and Lexis AI hallucinated 17% and 33% of answers respectively, the court issued its order explicitly as a warning to the entire Ninth Circuit bar.

Reporter's Take: This opinion is doing doctrinal work. The fabrication/inaccuracy taxonomy gives courts a framework to classify and escalate AI misconduct — and the 6-month suspension for the cover-up, not just the error, sends a clear message that how you respond when AI burns you matters as much as the initial mistake. Workers' comp and PI counsel filing in federal courts should read this opinion in full and show it to every associate and paralegal touching briefs.

🏛 ILLINOIS

IDHR'S JUNE 10 AI RULEMAKING HEARING POSTPONED — COMMENT WINDOW STILL OPEN THROUGH JUNE 29

The Illinois Department of Human Rights announced June 2 that its public hearing on the proposed Subpart J regulations implementing HB 3773 / Public Act 103-0804 — the Illinois AI Employment Opportunity Act — has been "temporarily postponed" from its June 10 date to allow for "continued collaboration with other state agencies." The 45-day public comment window, triggered by the May 15 publication in the Illinois Register, remains open through June 29, 2026.

The proposed Subpart J rules require employers who use AI in covered employment decisions — recruitment, hiring, promotion, discharge, discipline, RTW determinations, and terms and conditions of employment — to provide written notice to employees that AI is being used. The law prohibits use of AI that discriminates based on protected characteristics even if the discrimination is unintentional, and bars use of zip codes as proxy for protected classes.

WC/PI exposure: The direct WC relevance is through RTW and accommodation decisions. If a carrier or TPA uses AI to evaluate an injured worker's fitness for return to work or to make job classification decisions that affect benefit eligibility, that system may trigger HB 3773 notice and non-discrimination obligations. Employers using AI-driven IME review tools, claims triage systems, or disability management platforms should assess whether those systems influence covered employment decisions. Violations expose employers to actual damages, civil penalties, attorneys' fees, and compliance reporting.

Monitoring: Next steps unclear — IDHR may revise the proposed rules before reopening the comment period. Stakeholders should monitor the IDHR Legislative Updates page and submit comments by June 29 if the window is not extended.

The AI medical record review market has matured from novelty to infrastructure in 2026, with platforms like InPractice, Superinsight.ai, DigitalOwl/ChartSwap Insights, Supio, and Tavrn each carving differentiated positions. But a candid practitioner guide from Quilia raises a distinction that should inform how WC/PI attorneys use these tools: AI excels at factual extraction (MRI findings, treatment dates, provider interactions) but cannot reliably perform legal analysis (whether a finding supports the demand, how a jury will receive it, whether it survives a Daubert challenge).

The guide flags a specific WC-relevant caveat: cases with older providers, handwritten notes, or non-standard formats see significantly reduced AI extraction accuracy — a real-world limitation for the large share of WC files that originate with occupational health clinics and community providers outside major hospital systems. The implication is that AI-generated chronologies in WC cases require more attorney-level verification than in standard auto tort files with modern hospital records.

On the IME side, Superinsight.ai has specifically built a product line for IME physicians and QMEs, helping evaluators organize chronologies and flag inconsistencies before issuing opinions. This raises a new evidentiary question beginning to surface in depositions: did the IME physician use AI assistance in reviewing records, and if so, what was the platform and what did it flag? Plaintiff counsel should begin asking this in IME deposition prep.

◼ EDITOR'S DISPATCH
01

The Cover-Up Is Always Worse Than the Error

The Ninth Circuit was explicit: lesser sanctions might have applied if the attorneys had disclosed AI use and apologized at the first opportunity. They didn't — and the escalation doctrine that court articulated is now available to every federal judge facing a similar pattern. In WC/PI practice, where AI is touching briefs, demand letters, interrogatory responses, and expert reports, the professional obligation to disclose and correct AI errors immediately is no longer an ethical preference. It's a case-management survival rule.

02

OpenAI's Legal Hire Is a Commoditization Signal, Not a Threat

Jason Boehmig joining OpenAI to build "Codex for Legal" is not a threat to WC/PI-focused legal AI vendors. It's a signal that the generic layer of legal AI — research, drafting, document review — is being commoditized by platform players. That pressure actually helps the WC/PI-specific vendors who have built causation analysis, PPD calculation, RTW documentation, and IME review workflows. Domain depth becomes the moat. The attorneys who benefit most are those who already understand what the generic tools can't do.

03

Two AI Sanctions in One Week Is a Trend Line, Not a Coincidence

The 9th Circuit and the W.D. Tennessee both acted on AI misconduct within hours of each other on June 3 — and these are not isolated outliers. The judicial enforcement curve is steepening. Firms that haven't formalized AI verification protocols, attorney-review requirements, and disclosure practices are accumulating professional responsibility exposure at an accelerating rate. If your firm doesn't have an AI-use policy with teeth by the time this edition publishes, write one today.
