◼ ABOVE THE FOLD

ILLINOIS PASSES LANDMARK AI SAFETY ACT — MANDATORY THIRD-PARTY AUDITS OF FRONTIER MODELS HEADED TO GOVERNOR'S DESK

Illinois moved decisively into the national AI governance vacuum last week when the Illinois House of Representatives passed Senate Bill 315 — the Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act — by a unanimous 110-0 vote on May 27, sending the most aggressive frontier AI oversight law in the country to Governor J.B. Pritzker, who has publicly pledged to sign it. The Senate had cleared the bill 52–5. If enacted, SB 315 will require large frontier AI developers — companies whose global affiliates report more than $500 million in annual revenue — to publish annual safety and capability risk reports, disclose critical safety incidents within 72 hours, and, in a first for any U.S. jurisdiction, submit to mandatory annual independent third-party audits of their safety practices. Supporters of OpenAI and Anthropic backed the bill; major AI trade associations opposed it. Illinois would become the third state to regulate frontier models, joining New York's RAISE Act and California's SB 53 — but SB 315 goes further by making outside auditor verification a legal requirement rather than a voluntary commitment. Key compliance provisions take effect January 1, 2028, with Illinois Attorney General enforcement authority and civil penalties. Legal commentators are already invoking the "Sacramento Effect": because frontier developers are unlikely to build Illinois-only audit pipelines, SB 315's requirements may functionally become the national baseline regardless of federal inaction.

Reporter's Take: For WC/PI practitioners, the practical import is indirect but real — the AI tools shaping claim valuations, IME scheduling, medical record review, and settlement analytics are built on the very frontier models this law will audit. When those systems are externally verified for safety and catastrophic-risk controls, defense counsel and carrier AI deployments will carry a new layer of accountability that plaintiff attorneys can invoke in discovery and Daubert challenges alike.

IMAGING AI & CAUSATION: THE TIMELINE COMPRESSION EFFECT

Faster access to advanced imaging (MRI, CT) — now averaging 53% shorter lag than 2021 — changes the evidentiary landscape in WC/PI causation disputes. When imaging happens within days of injury rather than weeks, the pre-litigation medical record is more complete, pre-existing conditions are documented earlier, and the plaintiff's treating physician's causation opinion is harder to attack on the grounds that imaging was delayed or incomplete. Defense IME physicians who previously relied on delayed imaging to argue insufficient diagnostic workup face a stronger initial medical record. Expect AI-accelerated imaging timelines to make early causation stipulations more common — and early causation fights sharper.

◼ EDITOR'S DISPATCH

The Audit Trail Is Now the Battlefield

Every AI-assisted claims decision — reserve-setting, triage flagging, care authorization, litigation risk scoring — now generates a documented audit trail that didn't exist under purely human adjuster models. This is the most underappreciated structural shift in WC litigation: not that AI is making decisions, but that it's making documented decisions. Discovery practice in contested WC claims needs to evolve immediately to request AI system documentation, training data vintage, model version logs, and output records. The carrier's AI platform is no longer just an operations tool — it's a discovery target.

Illinois Is Writing the National AI Playbook

SB 315 joining HB 3773 (Subpart J employment AI rules) and the NICU Leave Act in the same legislative session signals that Illinois is systematically building the most comprehensive AI-adjacent employment and safety law framework in the country. For WC/PI practitioners licensed in Illinois, this is a competitive edge: you are operating in the most legally sophisticated AI governance environment outside California. When federal rules eventually arrive — and they will — they are likely to converge on the Illinois/California/New York framework. Learning this framework now is not compliance overhead. It's a five-year head start.
