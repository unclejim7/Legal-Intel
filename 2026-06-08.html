THE PI AI ARMS RACE IS NOW AI-TO-AI: MIGHTY DEPLOYS CONSUMER SETTLEMENT AGENT TO NEGOTIATE DIRECTLY AGAINST CARRIER AI

The information asymmetry that has defined personal injury claims handling for decades — carriers armed with proprietary data, injured claimants armed with a lawyer or nothing — is collapsing faster than the profession anticipated. Mighty, a New York-based legal technology company, has completed the first motor vehicle injury claim settlements handled end-to-end by an AI agent, without a lawyer and without human involvement on Mighty's side. The platform — free to consumers — collects accident reports, police reports, and medical records, generates a real-time valuation, then deploys its AI agent to submit a demand package and negotiate directly with the carrier's AI system. If a settlement is reached, the consumer keeps 100% of the proceeds. If the case is too complex for pre-suit resolution, Mighty's agent escalates to a vetted attorney in its network — and that attorney's contingency fee doesn't kick in until they improve on the offer Mighty's AI already obtained. CEO Joshua Schwadron described the model plainly: "A lot of personal injury cases are straightforward and shouldn't have a lawyer taking 33–40% when there's no legal work to be done." This is the logical endpoint of a trend Legal Intel has tracked all year: EvenUp (now valued at over $2 billion) crowdsourcing settlement data from 2,000+ plaintiff firms; CLARA Analytics training carrier models on millions of closed claims; and now consumer-facing AI agents removing the attorney as intermediary entirely for a category of cases that, by volume, represents the bulk of the PI market.

Reporter's Take: The threat to PI practitioners isn't that Mighty is going to replace trial lawyers. It's that Mighty correctly identifies the category of cases — the 90% of PI matters that settle pre-suit without meaningful legal work — and strips the contingency fee from them. The 10% of cases requiring genuine legal strategy become the entire market for PI attorneys going forward. Firms that can't articulate their differentiated value in that 10% are in structural trouble.

TEXAS SUPREME COURT TAKES UP AI DEPOSITION TRANSCRIPT FIGHT

The Texas Supreme Court agreed June 5 to hear a dispute over whether a nonstenographic deposition transcript generated using AI-driven voice recognition technology can be used in litigation, after a lower court struck the transcript and barred future depositions using the same method. The case raises the question directly: does AI transcription satisfy Texas's long-standing authorization of non-stenographic deposition recording, or does it introduce reliability problems that require per se exclusion? Why it matters: For WC/PI practitioners, AI deposition transcription is already in wide use via platforms like Skribe, Prevail, and Lexitas's Deposition Insights+. A Texas Supreme Court ruling setting exclusion standards will set a benchmark for evidence foundation challenges in other jurisdictions and directly affect cost-of-deposition math in high-volume WC litigation.

FEDERAL COURTS SPLIT ON AI WORK PRODUCT PROTECTION — FOUR RULINGS IN Q1 2026

Federal courts issued four significant and diverging rulings in Q1 2026 on whether AI-assisted litigation preparation is protected by attorney-client privilege or the work-product doctrine. The central split: Warner v. Gilbarco (E.D. Mich., Feb. 10, 2026) protected a pro se plaintiff's ChatGPT-assisted litigation materials as work product; United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y., Feb. 17, 2026) held that a criminal defendant's solo Claude AI sessions were not privileged because the tool was a third party and the communications weren't at counsel's direction. In June, the Texas Business Court added a nuanced middle position: non-lawyer principal's ChatGPT conversations protected as work product, but disclosure of which discovery materials were fed to the tool required. Why it matters: How attorneys and clients use AI — specifically whether AI is deployed at counsel's direction as part of the legal strategy — is now the decisive privilege variable. WC/PI firms need an AI-use policy before a discovery motion forces one.

Source: National Law Review / Akin Gump / White & Case

◼ THE AGENTIC CORNER

WHEN BOTH AGENTS DISAGREE: THE UNSOLVED PROBLEM AT THE CENTER OF AI-TO-AI CLAIMS NEGOTIATION

The Mighty model — consumer AI negotiating against carrier AI — raises a foundational question that neither the insurance industry nor legal technology has yet answered: what happens when two proprietary AI models reach opposing valuations and neither will move? Dan Ivtsan, writing in Insurance Thought Leadership, framed it directly: does a shared analytical baseline accelerate resolution because both sides converge on fair value, or does it entrench positions because each side treats its own model as authoritative truth?

The data divergence problem is structural. CLARA Analytics trains on carrier-side closed claims data — a dataset optimized around how carriers historically resolved claims, not how courts awarded them. EvenUp trains on plaintiff attorney settlement data — a dataset filtered through the negotiating behavior of plaintiff firms, which tends to anchor higher. When these two data architectures produce conflicting valuations and the negotiating agents both have authority to hold firm, the "straightforward" pre-suit case can deadlock faster than human negotiators would.

For WC/PI builders: The next infrastructure gap in the market is a trusted neutral AI valuation layer — a third-party model trained on actual verdict and resolution data, not party-specific negotiating data, that both sides can reference as a settlement anchor. This is the ADR infrastructure play of 2026–2027. The firm or startup that builds a defensible neutral AI verdict/settlement database for WC/PI claims will have leverage across both sides of the bar.

AI DEPOSITION TRANSCRIPTION: THE RELIABILITY QUESTION GOES TO THE TEXAS SUPREME COURT

The Texas Supreme Court's acceptance of the AI deposition transcript case forces a direct confrontation with the evidentiary reliability of AI voice recognition in legal proceedings. The stakes are significant for WC/PI practice: AI transcription platforms like Skribe, Prevail, and Lexitas Deposition Insights+ have gained wide adoption because they dramatically reduce deposition costs — in high-volume WC litigation where depositions of treating physicians, vocational experts, and IME doctors generate thousands of pages of transcript annually, cost compression is real. But if the Texas Supreme Court holds that AI transcripts require a foundation of human verification or per se fail the accuracy standard, the cost savings evaporate and the deposition service market resets. Watch this closely. A ruling restricting AI transcription will be cited in federal courts nationwide before the ink is dry.

AI-TO-AI CLAIMS: WHEN THE CARRIER'S MODEL SAYS ZERO AND MIGHTY'S SAYS $40,000

The structural problem with AI-to-AI PI settlement negotiation is that both models are trained on their own side's historical data. Carrier AI (CLARA, Gradient AI) is trained on closed-claims data that reflects how carriers have historically resolved claims — which includes the effect of prior information asymmetry and claimant underrepresentation. Plaintiff AI (EvenUp, Mighty) is trained on attorney-mediated settlement data anchored by firm-side demand patterns. Neither reflects actual jury verdict distributions for comparable injuries. The result: automated agent negotiations may converge on historically distorted "market" values rather than legally correct compensation. For plaintiff WC/PI attorneys, the response is to hold jury verdict data as an independent reference point — it's the one dataset neither carrier AI nor pre-suit settlement AI is trained on.

SURVEILLANCE AI IN WC CLAIMS — NAIC BIAS TESTING IMPLICATIONS

As carriers deploy AI-driven social media surveillance and activity monitoring for WC fraud detection, the NAIC Model Bulletin's bias testing requirements create an emerging obligation: carriers must test whether their surveillance AI flags claimants at disparate rates based on protected characteristics. An AI fraud detection model trained on historical claims data will inherit the racial, gender, and age biases embedded in historical adjuster decisions. Plaintiff attorneys should be asking in discovery not just "did you surveil my client with AI" but "what bias testing has been performed on your surveillance model and what were the results."

◼ EDITOR'S DISPATCH

The AI Privilege Doctrine Is Being Written Right Now

Four diverging federal court rulings in four months. The courts are working out the AI work-product doctrine in real time, and the variable that determines the outcome in every case is the same: was the AI used at counsel's direction, integrated into legal strategy, or deployed independently by the client? The practical implication is that attorney-directed AI use needs to be documented — not just performed. An email from counsel directing a paralegal to run medical records through an AI chronology tool, retained in the file, is a better evidentiary record than the same task performed without documentation. This is litigation hygiene, not paranoia.

Mighty Proves the Contingency Fee Is the Product, Not the Service

When a free AI agent can value and settle a straightforward motor vehicle injury claim with zero legal fees to the consumer, the contingency fee structure is revealed for what it always was: a pricing model for access, not a pricing model for legal complexity. The cases Mighty targets — the simple rear-enders, the soft-tissue claims with clear liability — were always administrative work priced as if they were legal work. The profession has known this for decades; Mighty is just the first product that makes the arbitrage explicit to consumers. The correct response is differentiation, not denial.

Illinois Has Six Months to Set the National AI Governance Standard

With SB 315 taking effect January 1, 2027, Illinois has a six-month window to define implementation — audit standards, incident reporting protocols, attorney general enforcement priorities — before any other state has to follow. The IAPP's assessment that Illinois, Connecticut, and New York are moving in concert suggests a tri-state governance cluster is coalescing around frontier AI oversight. If those three states align their audit standards, the "Sacramento Effect" applies: major AI developers will audit to the highest common standard, and that standard becomes national by default. Illinois practitioners should be engaged in that standard-setting now.

Legal Fact of the Day
The attorney-client privilege is the oldest evidentiary privilege recognized in the common law, predating American independence — English courts recognized it as early as the 16th century. Work-product doctrine, by contrast, is a distinctly American creation, established by the U.S. Supreme Court in Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947). The Ninth Circuit's 2026 AI sanctions order in LNU v. Blanche is now cited alongside Hickman in continuing legal education materials on professional responsibility.
