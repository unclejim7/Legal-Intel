◼ Medical & Expert Intelligence

AI Medical Record Review: Where It Helps and Where It Fails

A practitioner-level analysis published in April 2026 offers the clearest breakdown to date of what AI can and cannot do in medical record review for PI and WC cases. The framing is worth internalizing:

AI excels at: Building chronological treatment timelines, organizing billing records, and extracting structured data from provider notes — the "mechanical extraction" layer.

AI fails at: Judging whether an MRI finding supports or undermines a demand, assessing whether a treatment gap reflects claimant noncompliance or provider unavailability, and evaluating whether a diagnosis will survive IME challenge in a given jurisdiction. Those are judgment calls that depend on venue, jury expectations, and injury mechanism. AI-generated causation narratives need attorney review every time.

The practical implications for WC/PI teams: use AI to compress mechanical review from days to hours, but build explicit workflow gates requiring attorney sign-off on every causation conclusion and red-flag assessment before those outputs touch a demand, filing, or settlement number.

Nippon Life v. OpenAI Is the Illinois Case Every Carrier and Defense Firm Should Be Reading Right Now
The conventional framing of AI liability in legal contexts focuses on the attorney who files the hallucinated brief. Nippon Life flips that: here, an institutional plaintiff is suing the AI company itself, alleging it enabled unauthorized practice of law against the institution. If that theory survives a motion to dismiss, it opens a new litigation category — third-party institutional AI liability — with direct implications for carriers whose adversaries are using public AI tools to generate demands, file board complaints, or negotiate settlements. The damages theory (relitigation expenses, $10M punitive, injunction) is ambitious, but the standing and preemption battles will define the landscape regardless of outcome.
